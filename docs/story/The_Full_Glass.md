# The Full Glass

*The story of the universe according to TRPG, by Matthew Gelfman and Melissa Behrends-Gelfman. No mathematics is needed, and every chapter ends by saying how sure anyone can be.*

## A Note Before We Begin

This is a story about everything: where the universe came from, what it is made of, why things weigh what they weigh, why the stars shine, and why you, reading this, can only ever see the world from one place at a time.

It is told the way one particular theory tells it. The theory is called **Dynamic Relational Transitioning Geometry**, or TRPG for short. Matthew Gelfman began working on it in 1998. Melissa Behrends-Gelfman joined him in 2010, and since then it has been their joint work, with equal credit. It grew out of thought experiments and long, quiet thinking, and out of one stubborn question: *what if the great physicists were each right, but were looking at the same thing from different sides?*

You do not need any mathematics to read this book. Every idea comes with a picture you can hold in your head with your eyes closed. If you want the numbers, they are there, tucked into short boxes at the end of each chapter.

There is one promise this book makes, and it matters more than any other. **It will tell you honestly what is known, what is guessed, and what is still a mystery.** A theory of everything that hides its weak spots is not worth reading. So at the end of every chapter there is a box called **"How sure are we?"** It uses a handful of plain labels:

- **This follows from the theory.** It comes out of TRPG's own ideas.
- **Borrowed.** This is established physics that TRPG keeps exactly as it is. The credit belongs to the people named.
- **Measured.** A number taken from experiment, because the theory cannot yet produce it on its own.
- **Prediction.** The theory says it, and an experiment will soon check. *Not 100% until the measurement returns.*
- **A lead.** A promising idea that has not yet been shown.
- **A picture.** A way of seeing that fits what we know but has no numbers of its own yet.
- **Still a mystery.** A question the theory has not answered.
- **Not our question.** Something the theory deliberately does not try to explain.

One more thing about how this theory was built. It starts from existing conclusions. Any idea that disagreed with determined measurements was discarded, and the record of each one is kept. Its purpose is to show that the three great successes of physics, the Standard Model of particles, Einstein's gravity and quantum mechanics, already fit together, building on the work of the people who found them. Wherever a choice was made with today's measurements in view, it is labelled as fitted, not predicted. The theory's predictions are only for measurements that have not yet been made.

TRPG might turn out to be wrong. Every good theory must be able to be wrong, or it is not saying anything. If it does fail, the hope is that this book still does its job: it lets anyone, of any age and any background, understand what a theory of everything is trying to do, and see clearly where the hard questions are.

Let us begin where all stories begin.

# PART ONE: THE POND

## Chapter 1. In the Beginning

Close your eyes.

You are standing at the edge of a pond on a grey, windless day. Not a ripple moves. The surface is so still it looks like glass, so flat you could lay a ruler on it. There is no shore on the far side that you can see, no reeds, no bank. As far as you can look, there is only the pond.

A light drizzle is beginning to fall.

One drop comes down and touches the surface. A ring of tiny waves spreads out from the spot where it landed, wider and wider, smooth and even in every direction.

Here is the first thing to notice. Nothing new was added to the pond. The drop was water, and the pond was water. What moves outward in that ring is water that was already there, now carrying the energy of the drop as waves. The amount has not changed. It has only spread.

That ring is our universe. That drop is what we call the Big Bang.

Now look a little further. Far across the grey surface you can see other rings, from other drops, spreading on their own. Ours is one of many. It is not the centre of the pond, because the pond has no centre. It is simply where we happen to be.

This picture replaced an earlier one. For a long time the theory pictured the Big Bang as a water balloon bursting on concrete: one sudden splash, the same amount of water spreading out as waves from a single point. It was a good picture for showing that nothing is lost. But a balloon has an outside, a pavement beneath it and air around it, and the universe does not. The pond does better. A still surface gives us the flatness astronomers measure. A drizzle gives us many drops, so ours need not be special. And the tiny unevenness of rain on water will, later in this book, turn out to matter a great deal.

There is one more thing the pond teaches, and it may be the strangest. If you were a tiny creature riding on one of those waves, every direction around you would look the same. You would see the ring spreading away from you on all sides. You might think *you* were at the centre. So would every other creature on every other wave. The centre of our ring is not a place you could travel to. It is a moment: the moment the drop landed. That is exactly what astronomers see. Look in any direction, and the oldest light in the sky looks the same.

> ### How sure are we?
>
> - **The universe is one spreading ring in which nothing is created or destroyed, only changed in form.** This follows from the theory, and it agrees with everything measured.
> - **The "centre" is a moment, not a place.** This is borrowed: it is how standard cosmology already works (Friedmann, Lemaître, Robertson and Walker, 1920s and 30s). The pond is a picture of it.
> - **Other drops, other big bangs.** A picture. Nothing in the measurements rules it out.
> - **The universe is flat on average, like the pond.** Astronomers measure it flat to within about 0.2%. The theory agrees with this, but has not proved it must be so. A universe curved back on itself could also be "full". So: consistent, not derived.
> - **What made the drop fall, and what the pond is in.** Not our question. TRPG deliberately does not try to explain the cause of big bangs.

## Chapter 2. The Glass That Is Always Full

Close your eyes again.

You are holding a tall glass. Half of it is water. The top half is air.

Is it half empty?

Most people would say yes. But think about it for a moment. There is no empty part. The bottom half is full of water and the top half is full of air. The glass is completely full. It is simply full of two different things.

This is the first and deepest idea in the whole theory. **Space is never empty.** It is always full. What fills it can change from one form to another, but there is never a gap, never a hole, never a nothing.

Now hold a different picture. Imagine a sealed plastic bag, completely filled with liquid. No air bubble, no wrinkle, no space at all. Drop a marble into the liquid before you seal it. Is there a gap between the marble and the liquid? No. The liquid presses right up against it on every side.

Here is the twist. In the universe, the liquid **is** space itself. So there is not even a "between" for a gap to be in. Everything that exists floats in this full medium, pressed against it, part of it.

You might ask: if space is full, full of what? The honest answer is that it is full of everything that exists, in whatever form it takes at that place and time: matter, light, the energy of motion, and the strange steady energy of empty space that astronomers call dark energy. As the universe ages, the shares of these change. Light thins out fastest as space stretches. Matter thins more slowly. Dark energy stays steady. But at every moment, the shares add up to the whole. The glass is always full.

This is not as strange as it sounds. Modern physics already says something very like it. According to quantum physics, what we call "empty space" is never truly empty. Every kind of particle has a field that fills all of space, even where no particle is present, and those fields are always faintly trembling. Physicists measured that trembling long ago: two metal plates held very close together in a vacuum are pushed towards each other by it. TRPG takes this seriously, and says it plainly: space is a full medium.

There is one more consequence, and it is worth slowing down for. If the bag is always full and there is nothing outside it, then there is nowhere to stand to weigh the whole bag at once. You can check that nothing is lost in any one place, by watching carefully what flows in and what flows out. But you can never step outside the universe to put it on a scale. Conservation holds, but it can only be checked locally. That, too, is how physics already works under Einstein's general relativity.

> ### How sure are we?
>
> - **Space is a full medium with no gaps.** This follows from the theory. It is TRPG's first idea, and it matches what quantum physics already says about the vacuum.
> - **Energy is conserved, but can only be checked locally.** Borrowed: this comes from Emmy Noether's great theorem of 1918 and from Einstein's general relativity.
> - **The shares of matter, light and dark energy add up to the whole.** Borrowed from standard cosmology and measured by the Planck satellite (2018). Put into the standard equations, they give the universe an age of 13.79 billion years. The measured age is 13.787 billion, give or take 20 million years.

# PART TWO: THE FIRST FIRE

## Chapter 3. A Universe Made of Ties

Close your eyes.

The drop has just landed. The ring is tiny, unimaginably hot and packed tight. Nothing in it has a shape yet. No atoms, no particles you could point at, nothing settled.

Picture a crowd so dense that you cannot tell where one person ends and the next begins. Everyone is pressed against everyone else. In a crowd like that, it stops making sense to ask *who is where*. The only thing that makes sense is to ask *who is connected to whom, and how strongly*.

This is TRPG's second great idea. **The universe is made of relationships.**

Think about a family. A family is made of people, and each person is perfectly real. But the word "family" is not about the people one by one. It is about how they are connected: parent and child, brother and sister, cousin and aunt. Take away the connections and what you have left is not a family. It is just a crowd.

The universe is like that. Every particle is real. But what the universe *does*, every law it follows, comes from how its particles are connected to one another.

This is not a poetic flourish. It is how physics actually works. No one has ever measured anything by itself. You measure a length against a ruler, a weight against a standard weight, a time against a clock. Even "the weight of an electron" really means the electron compared with something else. Every measurement is a relationship.

So imagine writing it all down. Take every kind of particle in the universe and list them along the top of a huge table and down its side. In each square, write how the particle on that row relates to the particle in that column. Mathematicians call a table like that a *matrix*. Matthew and Melissa's insight is that this table, and the patterns in it, are the laws of nature.

The first of these tables, every particle against every other particle, has been built in full, with 171 pairings for the 19 known kinds of particle. Beside it sit tables for antiparticles, for the ways particles decay, and for the quarks and gluons inside the proton. The rest of this book is, in a sense, a tour of what those tables show.

> ### How sure are we?
>
> - **The universe is made of relationships; particles are real, but what they do comes from how they relate.** This follows from the theory. It is the heart of Matthew and Melissa's original work. The same philosophy appears in Carlo Rovelli's relational quantum mechanics (1996), who reached it independently.
> - **The particle-to-particle table.** Built and published with the theory's data. Anyone can check it.

## Chapter 4. The Only Number That Works

Close your eyes.

You have two ties in your hands, like two lengths of rope. Tie them together end to end and you have a longer rope. Simple.

Now imagine a rule. Whenever you join two ties together, two things must be true. First, the result must be a whole tie, with no holes in it. Second, nothing can be lost: the strength of the joined tie must be exactly what the two pieces bring.

That sounds like a gentle rule. It turns out to be one of the strictest rules in all of mathematics.

In 1898 a German mathematician named Adolf Hurwitz proved something remarkable. If you want a kind of number that you can multiply together with no holes and nothing lost, there are only **four** kinds in the whole of mathematics. There are the ordinary numbers you count with. There are the "complex" numbers, which have two directions. There is a kind with four directions, called the quaternions. And there is one with eight directions, called the **octonions**. That is the end of the list. There is no fifth.

If the full bag of Chapter 2 is made of relationships, and relationships combine with no holes and nothing lost, then the universe's ties must be written in one of these four kinds of number.

Which one? Here the theory asks nature. We know from experiment that there is a force in the universe richer than the weak force, the one that makes some atoms fall apart. That is the strong force, which holds the centres of atoms together. Only the largest of Hurwitz's four number systems has room for a force like that. So the answer is the octonions, the eight-directional numbers.

Now comes the beautiful part.

Close your eyes again and imagine you are standing inside that eight-directional space. You can only ever stand in one spot, and face one way. That is all any observer can do. The moment you pick your facing, the eight directions fall into a pattern. One direction is the one you are facing. Six are left around you, and they fall naturally into three pairs.

**Three pairs. Three colours.**

Physicists discovered in the 1960s and 70s that quarks, the tiny pieces inside protons, come in exactly three kinds of "colour charge". (It has nothing to do with real colour; physicists just borrowed the word.) They measured it. They never knew why it was three. In TRPG, three is what you get when you stand in an eight-directional space and face one way.

> ### How sure are we?
>
> - **Only four kinds of number keep ties whole with nothing lost.** This is borrowed mathematics: Hurwitz's theorem of 1898. It is proven.
> - **The octonions are the ones nature uses.** This follows from the theory, given one measurement: that a force richer than the weak force exists.
> - **Facing one way leaves exactly three colours.** This follows from the theory.

## Chapter 5. Standing in One Spot

Close your eyes.

You are standing in a field at night, holding a torch. You can point it anywhere, but only in one direction at a time. Whatever you are pointing at is lit up. Everything else is in shadow. If you turn around, a different part of the field lights up, and the first part goes dark.

This is TRPG's way of seeing every observer, everywhere: **we see one perspective at a time.** We stand in one spot and face one way.

Now take the great table of relationships from Chapter 3, written in the eight-directional numbers of Chapter 4, and look at it from one spot, facing one way. Something extraordinary happens. The table breaks into three pieces, of three very particular sizes: **eight, three and one**.

Physicists know these numbers by heart. They are the numbers of force carriers in nature.

- **Eight** gluons carry the strong force, which binds quarks together.
- **Three** carriers handle the weak force, which lets some atoms change and decay.
- **One**, the photon, carries the electric and magnetic force, which holds atoms together and brings you light.

The modern theory of particles, called the Standard Model, was built over half a century by some of the greatest minds in physics. It uses exactly this pattern of 8, 3 and 1. But it takes the pattern as a given, because that is what experiments showed. In TRPG, the pattern is what one table of relationships looks like when you see it from a single place.

Then Matthew had another idea. What if you looked at the relationships not two at a time, but **three** at a time? A relationship among three is richer than a pair. It is a triangle, not a line. Read the table that way, and out comes every particle of a whole family of matter, with exactly the electric charges experiments have measured. Up-type quarks with two thirds of a charge. Down-type quarks with minus one third. The electron with minus one. The neutrino with none. Even the partners that the two-at-a-time reading kept missing appear in their proper places.

And there is a third surprise, perhaps the most surprising of all.

For seventy years physicists have puzzled over a strange fact about the weak force: it only touches one "side" of particles. Particles spin, and the weak force only acts on those that spin one way relative to their motion, like a screw with only a left-handed thread. This is called handedness, and it has always looked like an arbitrary choice that nature made. Why left and not right?

Matthew's answer was simple. *It is the side you observe.* If every observer stands in one spot and faces one way, then one side is in the torchlight and the other is in shadow. The weak force touches the side you can see from where you stand. In TRPG, the word "handedness" is not needed. It is the perspective.

> ### How sure are we?
>
> - **One spot, one facing gives forces of sizes 8, 3 and 1.** This follows from the theory by a theorem: keep one spot and one facing, and the symmetry that is left has exactly these three sizes, whichever facing is chosen. The mathematics was worked out by Murat Günaydin and Feza Gürsey (1973), Ivan Todorov and Michel Dubois-Violette (2018) and Kirill Krasnov (2019), and TRPG reproduces it. The full list of a family's particles needs one further reading of the table, which is stated, not derived.
> - **Reading relationships three at a time gives a whole family of particles, with their measured charges.** This follows from the theory, with the same stated reading. The three-way idea is Matthew's.
> - **The weak force's one-sidedness is the side you observe.** This follows from the theory. The idea is Matthew's.
> - **How strong each force is.** Measured. The theory gives which forces exist and how they are shaped, but not their strengths. Three numbers come from experiment. (The next chapter tells of a bet that may save one of them.)

## Chapter 6. The Far Side of the Moon

Close your eyes.

You are looking at the Moon. It is bright and close and full of detail: craters, grey seas, mountains. But you only ever see one face of it. The Moon turns in step with its orbit, so the same side always faces the Earth. There is a whole other half up there, just as real, that no one on Earth ever sees directly.

The great table of relationships has a far side too.

Everything in the last chapter came from the part of the table that faces us: the forces, the colours, the families of particles. But part of the table faces away. And TRPG makes one bet about it, the only real bet in the whole theory: **the far side is real.**

On that far side sit some very heavy partners. There are three that carry no colour at all, one for each family of matter. Matthew named them the **MMG**, which stands for *Mass Mirrored by Gravity*. And there are heavier ones that do carry colour, which the theory simply calls **D**.

How heavy? Unimaginably. The MMG sit at an energy about 250 million times that of a proton. D sits far higher still. No machine on Earth will ever make them. The largest particle collider ever built falls short of the MMG by a factor of tens of thousands.

So how could anyone ever know they are there?

Here we need a new picture. Close your eyes once more and look at the pond's surface very, very closely. Closer. Closer still. At some point, the water stops looking smooth, the way a photograph breaks up into tiny dots when you zoom in far enough. That finest dot size is called the **grain of space**. It is far smaller than anything we have ever seen.

Now here is a curious fact about the three forces. Their strengths are not fixed. They change as you look more closely. The strong force grows weaker the closer you look; the electric force grows a little stronger. Physicists have known for decades that if you follow the three strengths all the way down towards the grain, they *almost* meet at one point. Almost, but not quite. It is like three roads that seem to be heading for the same crossroads, but miss each other by a few streets.

The far side changes that. If the MMG and D are real, they lean on the three roads as they approach the grain, and the three strengths meet. That meeting is not just pretty. It pins down one of the strengths exactly. TRPG says the strong force, measured at a standard reference point, must have a strength of **0.1178**.

That is a number an experiment can check. And it is checking it now.

There is one more thing the far side may do. The Higgs particle, discovered at CERN in 2012, has a weight that physicists have never been able to explain. In 2010, two physicists, Mikhail Shaposhnikov and Christof Wetterich, suggested a rule that might fix the Higgs weight at the grain of space. On its own, the rule gives a Higgs a little too heavy. With TRPG's far side included, it comes much closer: **127.2 GeV**, give or take 1.5, against a measured 125.2.

Notice something important about the bet. If it fails, if the strong force turns out not to be 0.1178 and the far side has to go, everything in the earlier chapters still stands. The three colours, the forces of 8, 3 and 1, the families and their charges: none of them depend on the bet. That is deliberate. A good theory does not hang everything on one hook.

And one more thing. The three forces *meet* near the grain, but they do not *merge* into a single force. This matters. Many theories have tried to merge all the forces into one. If you do, it turns out that protons should slowly fall apart. Huge underground experiments have watched for that for decades, and no proton has ever been seen to decay. TRPG's forces meet, shake hands, and stay themselves.

> ### How sure are we?
>
> - **The far side is real.** This is the theory's one bet. Matthew's decision is to keep it "until proof says to remove it".
> - **The strong force strength is 0.1178.** Prediction. It will be decided by computer calculations of the strong force (called lattice calculations) at a precision of about 0.3%. One honest warning: the exact size of the grain of space was chosen with today's measurements in view, so this is not yet a clean, blind prediction. The best measurement today is 0.11873, give or take 0.00056, which sits slightly above the prediction (1.7 standard deviations). *Not 100% until the measurement returns.*
> - **The Higgs weight is 127.2 ± 1.5 GeV.** Prediction, a narrow pass. It needs the top quark to weigh 171.6 GeV, and the top quark's measured weight (172.57 ± 0.29) is a little off from that. A sharper measurement of the top quark will decide it. *Not 100% until the measurement returns.*
> - **The forces meet but do not merge, so protons do not decay.** This follows from the theory's rule, *nothing extra*.
> - **Whether the early universe was ever hot enough to make the MMG and D.** Still a mystery.

## Chapter 7. The Dimples in the Rain

Close your eyes.

Back to the pond. The drop has landed and the ring is spreading. But look closely at the surface. It is not perfectly flat. The drizzle has left a faint pattern of tiny dimples across the water: a little higher here, a little lower there. The differences are minute, about one part in a hundred thousand, far too small to see by eye.

Nothing could look less important.

And yet those dimples are where every galaxy in the universe will one day come from. Over hundreds of millions of years, matter will slowly gather in the slight hollows. Gravity will pull more matter towards wherever there is already a little more. The dimples will grow into threads, the threads into clumps, and the clumps into galaxies, stars, planets, and eventually people. These faint unevennesses are called the **seeds of galaxies**.

Astronomers have measured them in exquisite detail, frozen into the oldest light in the sky. They know exactly how big the dimples are, and they know that the larger dimples are very slightly stronger than the smaller ones.

What they do not know for certain is where the dimples came from.

Most cosmologists believe that in the first tiny fraction of a second, the universe went through a sudden burst of expansion called **inflation**, which stretched tiny quantum jitters into these cosmic dimples. Inflation is a powerful idea. But it needs something extra: a new field, with its own rules, that exists for that purpose.

TRPG does not use inflation. That is an honest gap, and this book will not hide it. TRPG does not yet say where the seeds of galaxies came from. It takes their size and their tilt from measurement.

But not using inflation has a consequence that can be tested. If inflation happened, it should have shaken space itself, leaving faint ripples of gravity from the very first instant, imprinted on that oldest light. If TRPG is right and there was no inflation, those ripples should not be there.

> ### How sure are we?
>
> - **The seeds exist, with the size and tilt astronomers measure.** Measured: two numbers.
> - **Where the seeds came from.** Still a mystery. TRPG does not use inflation and has no replacement yet.
> - **No ripples of gravity from the first instant.** Prediction. It will be decided by the LiteBIRD satellite and the CMB-S4 telescopes. *Not 100% until the measurement returns.*

# PART THREE: THE BUBBLES

## Chapter 8. When the Soup Went Still

Close your eyes.

Imagine a huge pot of soup at a rolling boil. Everything is churning; nothing stays in one place. Now turn off the heat and wait. The bubbling slows. The surface settles. Somewhere in that settling, a level forms, the way water in a glass finds its height and stays there.

About a trillionth of a second after the drop landed, the young universe cooled enough for something like this to happen. A field that fills all of space, the **Higgs field**, settled into a steady level. Before it settled, nothing had weight. After it settled, everything that touches it had somewhere to balance.

To understand what that means, we need the most important picture in this book.

Close your eyes and picture a single soap bubble floating in the air.

What is a bubble? It is not a ball. A ball is solid all the way through. A bubble is something completely different. Inside, the air pushes outward. Outside, the thin skin and the air around it pull and press inward. The bubble sits exactly where the push and the pull balance.

Press on it gently, and the edge moves in. Let go, and it moves back out. The edge is not a wall. **The edge is the balance point.**

Now picture two bubbles touching. Their edges flatten where they meet and mush together, and each loses its perfect roundness. Picture a whole heap of bubbles in a sink: each one pressed against its neighbours, the whole heap a little smaller than the bubbles would be apart.

Matthew has always insisted on this: **particles are bubbles, never balls.** And here is what follows from it, perhaps the most beautiful idea in the theory.

**Mass is the balance between containment and leakage.**

Every structure in the universe is something pushing out held in by something pulling in. Where the two balance, you get a stable thing. And the energy sitting at that balance point is what we feel as weight. Mass is not a separate kind of stuff. It is what a balance looks like from the outside.

This changes how you see everything. Most of the energy in the universe is not mass at all. It is motion, light, the restless energy of fields. It only shows up as mass at those special places where something comes to a balance and holds.

For the smallest particles, the ones with no smaller pieces inside them, the balance is with the Higgs field itself, that settled level in the soup. Each particle has its own strength of tie to the Higgs field. Its weight is that tie strength times the level the Higgs settled at.

> ### How sure are we?
>
> - **Mass is the balance between containment and leakage.** This follows from the theory. It is TRPG's containment idea, which has been in Matthew and Melissa's work from the start.
> - **The Higgs field settles and gives particles their weight.** Borrowed: this was discovered by Peter Higgs, François Englert and Robert Brout in 1964, and the Higgs particle was found at CERN in 2012.
> - **The level the Higgs settled at (246 GeV).** Measured.
> - **Why the Higgs settles at all, and why at that level.** Still a mystery.
> - **How the Higgs tells the three families of particles apart.** Still a mystery, and the biggest single one in the theory. This one question holds the weights of the heavy quarks, the way quarks mix, and a small twist in the electron's family (next chapter). The open numbers here are tie strengths, distances from balance, not simply "masses that don't line up".

## Chapter 9. Three Bells on a Cone

Close your eyes.

Three bells hang in a row in an old tower: a small one, a middle one and a large one. Strike any one of them and the other two begin to hum softly, because they hang from the same beam and are tied together through it.

These are the three members of the electron's family. The small bell is the **electron**, the particle that fills the outside of every atom. The middle bell is the **muon**, about 200 times heavier. The great bell is the **tau**, about 3,500 times heavier than the electron.

For decades physicists have wondered why these three weigh what they do. They look random.

They are not.

In the early 1980s a Japanese physicist named Yoshio Koide noticed something astonishing. Take the three weights, do a simple calculation with them, and you get two thirds. Not roughly two thirds. Two thirds to better than one part in ten thousand. No one knew why.

TRPG sees it like this. Picture an ice-cream cone, tilted at exactly 45 degrees. Each bell has two kinds of ringing: the way it rings by itself, and the way it rings together with the other two. On that 45-degree cone, those two kinds of ringing are in perfect balance. The tie of each particle to itself and its ties to its family are exactly equal.

Put the three bells on that cone, turn the cone by a small, fixed twist, and the three weights fall out, all from a single scale, to an accuracy of 0.003%.

And that means you can predict one bell from the other two. Give the theory the electron and the muon, and it tells you the tau must weigh **1776.969** units (in the units physicists use, millions of electron-volts). Experiments today say the tau weighs very close to that. A new experiment in Japan, called Belle II, will soon measure it more precisely than ever before.

But here the honesty box has real work to do. The 45-degree balance works beautifully for the electron's family. It does **not** work for quarks. The theory tested it on quarks, hard, and it failed. So TRPG does not claim it as a law of all matter. For the electron's family the theory can now say why. The three members are one shape in three placements, and the most even split there can be between a bell's own ring and its ringing with the others, exactly half and half, is the 45-degree cone. Why quarks do not follow the same even split is not yet known.

> ### How sure are we?
>
> - **The electron, muon and tau sit on a 45-degree balance.** For the electron's family this follows from the theory: the three family members are one shape in three placements, and a perfectly even split between a particle's tie to itself and its ties to the others gives exactly 45 degrees. It fails for quarks, and the theory cannot yet say why. The relation was found by Yoshio Koide (published 1983). Its geometric form is due to Robert Foot (1994) and Carl Brannen (2006).
> - **The tau weighs 1776.969 MeV.** Prediction. It sits within one standard deviation of today's measurement. Belle II will decide. *Not 100% until the measurement returns.*
> - **The small twist and the overall scale.** Measured: two numbers.

## Chapter 10. The Ghost Seen From Three Sides

Close your eyes.

You are walking through a park at dusk, and in the middle of a lawn there is a strange statue. From where you first stand, it looks like a horse. Walk a quarter of the way around and it looks like a rider. Walk further and it becomes a flag rippling in the wind. It is one statue. It never changed. Only your view of it changed. And you can only ever see it from one side at a time.

That is a **neutrino**.

Neutrinos are the ghosts of the particle world. Trillions of them pass through your body every second, mostly from the Sun, and almost none of them touch anything. They come in three "flavours", and for decades physicists were puzzled to find that a neutrino that sets out as one flavour can arrive as another. In TRPG, this is not a mystery at all. The three flavours are three perspectives on one thing. As a neutrino travels, you simply catch it from different sides.

Now another picture. Imagine each neutrino sitting on one end of a seesaw. On the other end, out of sight on the far side of the great table, sits a very heavy hidden partner. The heavier the partner on the far end, the higher and lighter the neutrino sits on its own end. That is why neutrinos weigh so astonishingly little, millions of times less than even the electron.

Put the neutrinos on the same 45-degree cone as the electron's family, add the two spacings between their weights that experiments have already measured, and the theory gives every neutrino's weight. The lightest weighs **0.36** of a thousandth of an electron-volt. The next weighs **8.7**. The heaviest weighs about **50**. Their total is about **0.059** electron-volts.

These numbers are bold. They are being tested right now. A giant detector called JUNO, buried under a mountain in southern China, is measuring the neutrinos streaming from nearby nuclear power stations. Telescopes mapping the whole sky, such as DESI and Euclid, are weighing all the neutrinos in the universe at once, by watching how they smooth out the cosmic web. And two great experiments still being built, DUNE in America and Hyper-Kamiokande in Japan, will measure the timing angle at which neutrinos turn from one flavour to another. TRPG says that angle lies between 253 and 292 degrees.

> ### How sure are we?
>
> - **Neutrino flavours are perspectives on one particle.** This follows from the theory. The idea is Matthew's. It gives a known mixing pattern called TM1.
> - **Neutrino weights of 0.36, 8.7 and about 50 thousandths of an electron-volt, in "normal" order, with a total of about 0.059.** Prediction. It will be decided by JUNO, DESI and Euclid. The normal order holds given today's limits from cosmology; the rule also allows a reversed order, but cosmology already rules that one out. *Not 100% until the measurement returns.*
> - **A mixing angle (sin²θ₁₂) of 0.318 and a timing angle of 253° to 292°.** Prediction. It will be decided by JUNO, DUNE and Hyper-Kamiokande. *Not 100% until the measurement returns.*
> - **A very rare decay (neutrinoless double beta decay) at a strength of 1.4 to 4.1 thousandths of an electron-volt.** Prediction, but beyond the reach of any experiment now planned.
> - **The seesaw.** Borrowed: proposed by Peter Minkowski (1977), and by Murray Gell-Mann, Pierre Ramond, Richard Slansky and Tsutomu Yanagida. In TRPG the heavy partners sit on the far side of the table.

## Chapter 11. The One Grain in a Billion

Close your eyes.

Picture a beach as long as a coastline, made of black sand and white sand poured in exactly equal amounts. Every time a black grain touches a white one, both vanish in a tiny flash of light. Grain by grain, the beach disappears, until nothing is left but light.

Except that it was not exactly equal. For every billion black grains, there were a billion and one white ones. When all the pairs have flashed away, those single leftover white grains remain.

Everything you have ever touched, every star, every planet, every person, is made of the leftover grains.

The black grains are **antimatter**: particles exactly like ordinary ones, but opposite in charge. When matter and antimatter meet, they turn into pure energy. In the first fire of the universe there was almost exactly as much of one as the other. Almost. Why there was that tiny excess of matter is one of the great unanswered questions in physics.

TRPG does not answer it either. The theory allows the excess, but does not predict it. The size of it is taken from measurement.

There is a lead. If the heavy hidden partners on the far side of the neutrino seesaw started out cold, their settling could produce exactly this kind of excess. But that has not been shown, and it costs extra numbers to describe. One idea that was tested and failed: that antimatter simply ran colder than matter. Except perhaps in small hidden pockets, that idea does not hold.

> ### How sure are we?
>
> - **One extra grain of matter in a billion.** Measured. TRPG allows it but does not predict it.
> - **A cold start for the hidden partners could make it.** A lead.
> - **"Antimatter ran colder."** Tested; it fails, except perhaps in small cold pockets.

## Chapter 12. Three Dancers Who Never Let Go

Close your eyes.

Three dancers stand in a ring, holding hands. Each wears a different coloured shirt: one red, one green, one blue. Together, from a distance, their colours blend into white. They can spin and sway and stretch, but there is one rule they can never break. They cannot let go.

Try pulling one dancer out of the ring. The joined hands stretch, like a rubber band. Pull harder, and they stretch further, and the harder you pull, the harder they pull back. Pull hard enough and the band finally snaps. But the energy you poured into stretching it does not disappear. It becomes a brand-new pair of dancers, who immediately join hands. You never end up holding one dancer alone.

These dancers are **quarks**, and their coloured shirts are the three colours of Chapter 4. The ring of three is a **proton** or a **neutron**, the particles at the heart of every atom. About ten millionths of a second after the drop landed, the universe had cooled enough for quarks to gather into these rings, and they have never let go since.

And now the bubble returns.

A proton is a bubble too. Inside, the quarks are squeezed into a tiny space, and anything squeezed into a tiny space jitters. It cannot sit still. That jitter is the push outward. The strong force, carried by gluons, is the pull inward. The edge of the proton is where the two balance.

Here is the astonishing thing. If you add up the weights of the three quarks inside a proton, you get about one percent of the proton's weight. One percent! The other ninety-nine percent is the energy of the push and pull itself, the balance energy of the bubble. You are made almost entirely of balance.

TRPG takes this one step further. Using only two measured facts, the weight of the proton and the weight of its slightly heavier cousin called the Δ (delta), the theory works out the pressure of the balance inside the proton: about **60** units (millions of electron-volts per cubic femtometre). Hold on to that number. In Chapter 18 it will travel, unchanged, into the heart of a dying star.

What about the quarks themselves? There are six kinds, called up, down, strange, charm, bottom and top. TRPG finds the **down** quark's weight from a single square of its tables, the one that says how much down-type quarks mix with each other (an angle named after the Italian physicist Nicola Cabibbo). It comes out at 4.76 units against a measured 4.70. The **up** quark can be reached from two squares of the tables as well, giving 2.08 against 2.16. That route trades one measured number for another, so it counts as a cross-check rather than a saving. The other four quarks still have to be measured. Their weights belong to the great open question of Chapter 8: how the Higgs tells the families apart.

> ### How sure are we?
>
> - **Quarks come in three colours.** This follows from the theory (Chapter 4).
> - **Quarks are always confined.** Borrowed: the theory of the strong force, quantum chromodynamics (David Gross, Frank Wilczek and David Politzer, 1973).
> - **A proton is a bubble whose edge is the push-pull balance, at a pressure of about 60 (57 to 64).** This follows from the theory's containment idea. The mathematics is the "bag model" of Alan Chodos, Robert Jaffe, Kenneth Johnson, Charles Thorn and Victor Weisskopf (1974). Experimenters have since measured the pressure inside the proton directly (Volker Burkert, Latifa Elouadrhiri and François-Xavier Girod, 2018).
> - **About 99% of a proton's weight is balance energy.** This follows from the theory and agrees with measurement. (If you also count the indirect effect of the heavy quarks, the figure is still about 71%.)
> - **The down quark's weight from the Cabibbo square.** This follows from the theory, and it saves one measured number.
> - **The up quark from two squares.** A cross-check, not a saving.
> - **The strange, charm, bottom and top weights, and how quarks mix.** Measured.
> - **The strong force's "angle"** (why it treats the flow of time so evenly). Measured. Every attempt to explain it so far has failed.

## Chapter 13. Through the Eyes of a Proton

Close your eyes.

An ant and an eagle are looking at the same field. The ant sees towering green blades, a forest of grass, each blade as tall as a tree. The eagle, high above, sees a smooth green patch. Which of them is right?

Both are. Each sees the same field at its own size.

TRPG says every particle "sees" the universe at its own size. And we, being built of protons, see the universe at the proton's size. Atoms, chemistry, light, the solid feel of a table: all of it lives at the proton's scale. That is why the world looks the way it does to us. The quarks, far smaller, barely show in everyday life, just as the eagle never sees a single blade of grass.

Be careful with this idea, though, because it is easy to misread. It does not mean the proton is the same thing as a star, or that the universe is a giant proton. It means the proton is the scale our view is set at. It is the size of our window.

There is an old riddle here. In the 1930s the astronomer Arthur Eddington and the physicist Paul Dirac noticed that if you measure the universe in proton-sized units, some enormous numbers keep turning up. The universe holds about 10⁸⁰ protons' worth of matter. Gravity between two protons is weaker than their other forces by about 10³⁸. These huge numbers seem to echo each other in strange ways, and no one has ever explained why. In TRPG, the proton's numbers are the ones we notice because the proton sets our window.

And Matthew adds a warning that runs through this whole book. The biggest confusion in physics, he says, is not about whether things exist. It is **mixing up scales** when we think about them. A crowd seen from an aeroplane is one smooth patch; walk into it and you see people. Neither view is wrong. But you must not mix them.

> ### How sure are we?
>
> - **Each particle sets its own scale view, and ours is the proton's.** A picture: Matthew and Melissa's scale-view idea.
> - **In proton units the universe holds about 5.5 × 10⁷⁹ protons' worth of matter, matching the large-number riddle.** Consistent with Eddington (1930s), Dirac (1937) and Steven Weinberg (1972). Whether the laws of nature single out the proton, or the proton is just one rung on a smooth ladder, is still a mystery.

## Chapter 14. The First Three Minutes

Close your eyes.

The proton bubbles are crowded close together now, jostling in the still-hot young universe. Most of the time they bounce off each other. But now and then two collide hard enough that they stick. Their edges flatten and mush together, the way two soap bubbles merge in a sink. And as they settle into their new, snugger shape, a little of their balance energy is let go as a flash.

In the space of about three minutes, a quarter of all the ordinary matter in the universe settles into helium. Almost all the rest stays as hydrogen, with tiny traces of a few other light elements. Then the universe cools too much for any more sticking to happen, and the mix is frozen in. Astronomers measure that mix in the oldest stars and gas clouds today, and it matches.

The bubble picture shows its strength here. Physicists discovered in 1983, to their surprise, that protons and neutrons packed inside a nucleus are measurably different from free ones. It was called the EMC effect, after the European Muon Collaboration that found it. In the bubble picture, that is exactly what you would expect. Squash bubbles together and they change shape.

The same picture explains why splitting a heavy atom releases energy. When a big nucleus splits in two, the pieces settle into balances that hold more snugly, and the difference is let go. Nothing is destroyed. The balance has simply moved. TRPG's estimate of that released energy, 166 units, comes close to the 173 that is measured: near, but not exact.

> ### How sure are we?
>
> - **About 25% helium, the rest hydrogen, plus traces.** Borrowed: the theory of the first nuclei, by Ralph Alpher, George Gamow and others, and it matches measurement.
> - **Packed bubbles change shape (the EMC effect).** This follows from the bubble picture.
> - **Fission lets go of balance energy; nothing is destroyed.** This follows from the theory. The estimate (166 against 173 measured) is close but not exact.

## Chapter 15. The Fog Lifts

Close your eyes.

For hundreds of thousands of years, the universe is a glowing fog. It is like being inside a lamp shade: everywhere is bright, but you cannot see through it. Light can only travel a short way before it bumps into a loose electron and bounces off in a new direction.

Then the fog cools enough for each electron to settle into place around a nucleus, forming a bubble of its own: the first atoms. Suddenly there are no loose electrons left to bump into. The fog clears. The light that was trapped streams out freely in every direction, and it has been travelling ever since.

That happened about 380,000 years after the drop landed. And we can still see that light today. Space has stretched so much since then that the light has been stretched too, from a white-hot glow into faint microwaves. In 1965 two radio astronomers, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, picked it up as an annoying hiss in their antenna that would not go away. It was the afterglow of the beginning.

It is almost perfectly even: the same in every direction, to one part in a hundred thousand. And in those tiny differences, astronomers can see the dimples of Chapter 7, the seeds of galaxies, caught in the light like ripples frozen in ice.

# PART FOUR: THE LIGHTS IN THE SKY

## Chapter 16. Threads That Pull Together

Close your eyes.

Place a coin between two mirrors, so that one mirror shows heads and the other shows tails. Now cover one mirror. Glance at the other. It shows heads. Instantly you know, without looking, that the covered one shows tails.

Did a message travel from one mirror to the other? Of course not. It was always one coin, seen from two places.

Particles can be linked like that. Physicists call it **entanglement**. Two entangled particles behave as one thing, however far apart they are. Measure one, and you know about the other at once. In 1935 Einstein found this so unsettling that he called it "spooky action at a distance". Erwin Schrödinger, the same year, said it was not just one feature of quantum physics; it was *the* feature.

TRPG gives it a picture. **Entangled particles touch through the geometry.** There is no distance to cross, because in the shape of things, they are not apart. Nothing travels faster than light, because nothing travels at all.

Now picture every particle in the universe linked to others by threads like that, a vast, fine web stretching everywhere. Wherever there is more stuff, there are more threads. And the web draws together where the threads are thickest.

That drawing-together is **gravity**.

In TRPG, gravity is not a separate force laid on top of everything else. It is what all the entanglement in the universe adds up to. This is a bold-sounding claim, but it rests on solid ground. In the 1980s and 90s, physicists showed that in a medium that is full, like the full bag of Chapter 2, the amount of entanglement across any boundary grows with the boundary's area. Then, in 1995, a physicist named Ted Jacobson showed something remarkable: if you start from that fact, and add one rule from the science of heat, you can derive Einstein's equations of gravity. Gravity falls out of entanglement in a full medium.

There is a second picture, too. Shine a torch beam into a fish tank. Where it enters the water, the beam bends, because light travels more slowly in water than in air. Near anything heavy, space behaves the same way. It resists a little more. So light slows and bends as it passes. When starlight grazes the edge of the Sun, it bends by a tiny, exact amount, and astronomers have measured that amount: 1.75 seconds of arc, exactly what this picture gives. For it to work, the extra resistance must act on time and on space equally. Make it act on time alone, and the bending comes out only half as big.

What TRPG does not yet give is gravity's *strength*. Gravity is fantastically weak: a fridge magnet can hold a paperclip up against the pull of the entire Earth. Why gravity is so weak is tied, in TRPG, to the strengths of the other forces at the grain of space, and that is still a mystery.

There is one last thing to say about the coin and the mirrors. In 1964 John Bell proved that if entangled particles were secretly carrying instructions, like the coin having been heads all along, there is a limit to how closely their measurements could agree. In 1982 Alain Aspect and his colleagues tested it. The particles beat the limit. There are no hidden instructions. TRPG's answer is that the particles are not following instructions at all. They are one thing, touching through the geometry. In this view, nature does not roll dice at the bottom. It is connected.

> ### How sure are we?
>
> - **Gravity is a byproduct of entanglement; its form follows from a full medium.** This follows from the theory, through Jacobson's derivation (1995), resting on the area law (Luca Bombelli, Rabinder Koul, Joohan Lee and Rafael Sorkin, 1986; Mark Srednicki, 1993).
> - **Light bending by 1.75 seconds of arc and the radar echo delay.** Borrowed: Einstein's general relativity (1915), tested by Arthur Eddington (1919) and Irwin Shapiro (1964). TRPG's resistance picture reproduces them exactly.
> - **How strong gravity is.** Measured. Why it has that strength is still a mystery.
> - **Entangled particles beat any "hidden instructions" limit.** Borrowed: John Bell (1964), Alain Aspect and colleagues (1982). "Touching through the geometry" is TRPG's picture of it, close in spirit to David Bohm's view of 1952.

## Chapter 17. Bubbles That Leak

Close your eyes.

Millions of years pass. The gas of the young universe drifts into the slight hollows left by the drizzle. Gravity draws it in, more and more of it, until great clouds collapse and their centres grow hot. Hotter. Hot enough to glow. The first stars switch on.

Now watch the balance.

A star is a bubble whose push is winning, just slightly. Its heat pushes outward. Its own gravity pulls inward. But the push wins by a little, and so energy leaks out of it, steadily, for billions of years, as light and heat. That leak is why stars shine. A star is **under-contained**.

A black hole is the opposite. Its pull wins completely. Nothing gets out, not even light. A black hole is **over-contained**.

And a proton sits exactly in between. Push and pull in perfect balance. It neither leaks nor collapses. It simply lasts.

**It is the same rule at every size.** Push against pull. Only the forces doing the pushing and pulling change from one size to the next. In a star, it is heat against gravity. In a proton, it is quark jitter against the strong force. The rule does not care.

There is a simple number that measures how tightly anything is contained, called its compactness. For the Sun it is about two millionths. For a neutron star, about one fifth. For the last possible stable star, four ninths. For a black hole, one half. It is one ladder, from leaking to holding.

Matthew has a picture of the Sun itself that is worth telling, though it is a picture and not yet a calculation. At the Sun's very centre, he suggests, there is a particle that is, or acts like, a singularity: something with an enormous pull. But its containment is not quite strong enough, so it leaks. What surrounds it, the hot gas we see, is "gas" only at our scale. At its own scale, it is particles too, bursting outward from that leaking centre. Keep the two scales apart, he says, and the picture makes sense. Mix them, and it does not.

> ### How sure are we?
>
> - **Stars leak, black holes hold, protons balance: one rule at every size.** This follows from the theory's containment idea, using borrowed star physics (Richard Tolman, Robert Oppenheimer and George Volkoff, 1939; Hans Buchdahl, 1959).
> - **Fusion powers the Sun's core.** Borrowed: Hans Bethe (1939), confirmed by the solar neutrinos caught by the Borexino experiment.
> - **A leaking, singularity-like particle at the Sun's centre.** A picture, and a hypothesis. Nothing rules it out, and careful study of the Sun's vibrations near its core could test it. No calculation has been written for it yet.

## Chapter 18. The Heart of a Dying Star

Close your eyes.

A giant star has burned for millions of years, and now its fuel is gone. With nothing left to push outward, its core collapses under its own weight in less than a second. Matter crushes inward with unimaginable force.

The proton bubbles are pressed together harder than anywhere else in the universe outside a black hole. Their edges flatten. Then they vanish. The bubbles merge into one great bubble of quarks, about the size of a city, heavier than our Sun. This is a **neutron star**.

Deep inside, something remarkable happens. The quarks, packed so close, begin to pair up, holding hands two by two. Those pairs stiffen the star, so it can resist its own crushing gravity a little better.

Now remember the number from Chapter 12: the pressure of about 60 that holds a single proton together. TRPG takes that very same number, unchanged, with no new adjustments, and carries it into the heart of the neutron star.

Without the quark pairing, the number fails. The stars it produces are too soft, and they cannot reach the weights we actually observe. Astronomers have weighed neutron stars of about two times the Sun.

With quark pairing, it works, but only if the pairs hold on with a particular strength: an energy called the pairing gap, of about **82 to 97** units. That is TRPG's prediction. Not the weight of the stars, which astronomers already know, but how tightly the quarks inside them must pair.

With that gap, the heaviest neutron stars come out at about 2.0 to 2.15 times the weight of the Sun. One run of the theory's own calculator gives a star of 2.07 Suns, 11.09 kilometres across, which sits right in the window astronomers observe, both from the heaviest pulsars and from the faint squeeze seen when two neutron stars spiral together and merge.

Here the honesty box must be especially careful, because this is where the theory could break soonest.

> ### How sure are we?
>
> - **The same pressure that holds a proton is carried into neutron stars, with no new knobs.** This follows from the theory.
> - **The quarks in the core must pair with a gap of about 82 to 97 MeV.** Prediction. It will be decided by pulsars, neutron-star mergers and theory. Quark pairing itself is borrowed: Mark Alford, Krishna Rajagopal and Frank Wilczek (1998). At the central pressure of 60.1, a gap of 82 gives a heaviest star of 2.0 Suns and a gap of 97 gives about 2.13; across the proton's whole range of pressure the gap runs from 75 to 108. *Not 100% until the measurement returns.*
> - **The heaviest neutron stars weigh about 2.0 to 2.15 Suns.** This window is what astronomers observe, so the theory is consistent with it. The real prediction is the gap.
> - **What would break it.** A neutron star clearly heavier than about 2.15 Suns. Two to watch: the fast-spinning pulsar PSR J0952−0607 (2.35 Suns, give or take 0.17) and the strange object in the gravitational-wave event GW190814.

## Chapter 19. The Hidden Pull of Galaxies

Close your eyes.

You are standing far out in space, watching a great spiral galaxy turn. Hundreds of billions of stars swirl around its bright centre. And there is something wrong.

The stars near the edge are moving too fast.

Picture children on a merry-go-round. Spin it slowly and they hold on easily. Spin it too fast and they fly off. By the amount of visible matter in the galaxy, its outer stars are going so fast that they should fly away into space. They do not. Something unseen is holding them in. Astronomers call it **dark matter**, and they measure about five times as much of it as all the ordinary matter in the universe.

What is it? No one knows. But TRPG offers two pictures, and they may both be part of the answer.

The first picture: remember that space is a full medium with a baseline resistance. Where space is emptier, in the great voids between galaxies, the resistance is lower, and the voids gently push outward. In the outskirts of a galaxy, that changes how stars move. This picture works remarkably well for single galaxies. It reproduces a simple rule that galaxies have been found to follow, discovered by Mordehai Milgrom in 1983. It even fits the discovery in 2017 of a huge empty region, the Dipole Repeller, that seems to push our own galaxy away from it.

But it does not work everywhere. When two clusters of galaxies collided in the Bullet Cluster, the hidden mass sailed right through, separate from the visible gas, like ghosts walking out of a car crash. That cannot be resistance alone. Some of dark matter must be real stuff.

The second picture: imagine many identical, gentle waves lying on top of one another so perfectly that they merge into one smooth wave. From a distance, it is camouflage. It looks like nothing at all, yet it has weight. Physicists have studied this idea seriously, and very light particles really do behave like this.

The great table allows exactly one candidate for this kind of dark matter: a cold, chargeless, gentle wave left behind when the heavy hidden neutrino partners of Chapter 10 settled into their places. It is called a **majoron**. But the theory cannot yet say how much it weighs or how much of it there is. So it remains a lead, not part of the theory's core.

> ### How sure are we?
>
> - **How much dark matter there is (about five times ordinary matter).** Measured.
> - **Dark matter is partly a perspective of space's resistance.** This holds for single galaxies (Milgrom's rule, 1983; the Dipole Repeller, 2017). It fails for the Bullet Cluster, for the afterglow and for galaxy clusters, so part of dark matter must be real stuff. Matthew's resistance reading allows both.
> - **A cold, chargeless wave (the majoron) as the real part.** A lead, outside the theory's core. Its weight and amount are not derived. Overlapping identical waves really do merge (wave dark matter, Lam Hui and colleagues, 2017).
> - **What would break it.** Dark matter that feels the weak force.

## Chapter 20. The Foam of the Cosmos

The galaxies are not scattered evenly. They lie along thin threads and wide sheets, wrapped around enormous empty bubbles, like the foam on top of a bath. On one of those worlds, the atoms joined into longer and longer chains. Chemistry became biology. Biology became life. And life, after a very long time, became curious.

# PART FIVE: THE WATCHERS

## Chapter 22. One Window at a Time

Close your eyes.

You are standing on that small world, at night, looking up at the stars. You are made of the leftover grains of Chapter 11, built into proton bubbles in Chapter 12, wrapped into atoms when the fog lifted in Chapter 15, gathered by the threads of gravity in Chapter 16, cooked inside long-dead stars in Chapter 17, and pressed into a planet in Chapter 21.

And you can only ever see the universe from one place, facing one way.

That is not a weakness of human eyes. It is how the universe works for every observer. Werner Heisenberg discovered in 1927 that if you measure where something is very sharply, you lose track of how it is moving. And if you measure its motion very sharply, you lose track of where it is. Physicists call this the uncertainty principle.

TRPG reads it like this: position and motion are not two separate facts about a particle. They are two views of one geometry. You can turn your torch to light up one side or the other, but never both at once. We see one perspective of a single geometry at a time.

And that geometry never stops moving. Go back to the pond one last time. Drop two pebbles in at once. Where their ripples cross, the water forms little peaks, little dips and little saddles, and the pattern shifts and changes from place to place and from moment to moment. Yet the pond as a whole stays perfectly level. Mathematicians can prove that for a simple surface of crossing waves, every patch holds one peak, two saddles and one dip, and all the curving adds up to exactly zero. Always changing up close; flat on average.

That constant change is the word in the middle of the theory's name. **Dynamic**: the geometry keeps changing. **Relational**: what matters is how things relate. **Transitioning**: the curves keep moving between peaks, saddles and dips, while averaging to flat. **Geometry**: it is all one shape, seen from different perspectives.

There is one deep question here that TRPG has not answered. Quantum physics deals in probabilities: it tells you how likely each result is, but not which one you will get. Max Born gave the rule for those probabilities in 1926, and it has never failed. TRPG borrows that rule. If it could one day derive it from the geometry, then "nature does not roll dice" would become a result, not a hope. That day has not yet come.

> ### How sure are we?
>
> - **Uncertainty means we see one perspective of a single geometry at a time.** Borrowed result (Heisenberg, 1927; Schrödinger, 1935). The reading follows from the theory.
> - **Crossing waves: always changing, flat on average.** This follows mathematically for the model surface (the Gauss–Bonnet theorem) and agrees with the measured flatness of space. An equation for how the real geometry moves has not yet been written.
> - **Where quantum probabilities come from.** Borrowed for now (Max Born, 1926). Still a mystery.
> - **Run time backwards and flip charge and mirror image too, and physics is unchanged; flip time alone, and it is not.** Borrowed: the CPT theorem. It also holds in TRPG's tables.

# EPILOGUE: HOW THIS STORY COULD BE WRONG

Every good story has an ending. A good scientific story has something better: a way to find out whether it is true.

Here are the places where nature will soon answer back. Each of these follows from the theory. **None of them is 100% until its measurement returns.**

| The theory says | Who will check |
|---|---|
| Neutrinos weigh 0.36, 8.7 and about 50 thousandths of an electron-volt, in normal order, totalling about 0.059 | JUNO; the DESI and Euclid sky surveys |
| The neutrino mixing number sin²θ₁₂ is 0.318, and the timing angle is 253° to 292° | JUNO; DUNE; Hyper-Kamiokande |
| The tau weighs 1776.969 MeV | Belle II |
| Quarks in neutron-star cores pair with a gap of about 82 to 97 MeV | Pulsars, neutron-star mergers, and theory |
| The strong force's strength is 0.1178 | Lattice calculations at about 0.3% |
| The Higgs weighs 127.2 ± 1.5 GeV, if the top quark weighs 171.6 GeV | A sharper measurement of the top quark |
| A triangle of quark angles has γ between 64.1° and 67.6°, and a small shortfall in quark mixing shrinks to nothing | LHCb; Belle II |
| There are no ripples of gravity from the first instant | LiteBIRD; CMB-S4 |
| Protons never decay | Hyper-Kamiokande |

And here is what would break the theory outright:

- a new force carrier, a mirror particle, or a fourth family of matter;
- a proton seen to decay;
- neutrinos found in reversed order;
- a neutron star clearly heavier than about 2.15 Suns;
- dark matter that feels the weak force.

If any of these happens, the theory will have to change or fall. That is not a failure of science. It is science working. As Matthew wrote: *"Most of physics was wrong, but through constant analysis and thought experiments and trials and errors it became physics."*

## What the Story Deliberately Leaves Out

This list matters as much as everything else. Each item is something other theories of everything add, and TRPG does without.

- **No extra dimensions.** Three of space and one of time are enough.
- **No hidden or parallel universes.** Nothing exists that you could never, even in principle, send a message to. Other drops on the pond are in the same pond.
- **No strings, and no vast landscape of possible universes.**
- **No "superpartner" for every particle** at energies we can reach. (The MMG has a distant cousin in one such theory, but TRPG adds only what its own table already holds.)
- **No fourth family, no new forces, no mirror particles, and only one Higgs pair.** This is the theory's single rule: *nothing extra*.
- **No single merged force, and no decaying protons.** The forces meet but do not merge.
- **No inflation.** That is why the seeds of galaxies are honestly marked as a mystery, and why the theory predicts no ripples of gravity from the first instant.
- **No explanation of what caused big bangs.** That is not TRPG's question.
- **No re-deriving of chemistry, biology or engineering.** They rest on physics that TRPG keeps exactly.

## What Came From Matthew and Melissa, and Held Up

Every one of these was tested hard, under the rule that anything which can be shown wrong fails.

- **Relationships, not things.** One table of relationships, seen from one spot facing one way, gives which forces exist (8, 3 and 1) and three colours.
- **The relationship among three.** Reading the table three at a time gives a whole family of particles with their measured charges, including the partners that pairs alone missed.
- **The perspective replaces handedness.** The weak force's one-sidedness is the side you observe.
- **Neutrino flavours are perspectives.** This gives the mixing pattern and a timing angle for DUNE and Hyper-Kamiokande to test.
- **Mass is a balance.** It gives the proton's inner pressure from the proton and the Δ alone, and carries that same number into neutron stars to predict how tightly their quarks pair.
- **Particles are bubbles, never balls.** Packed bubbles change shape, as the EMC effect shows; neutron-star cores are merged bubbles.
- **The down quark from the Cabibbo square** (4.76 against 4.70), and the up quark from two squares (2.08 against 2.16) as a cross-check.
- **The MMG, "Mass Mirrored by Gravity".** The missing far-side body that makes the forces meet, and gives the strong force a definite predicted strength.
- **The still pond in the drizzle.** Flat on average, many bangs, no centre in space.
- **Dark matter as partly perspective.** It holds for single galaxies.

## The Bookkeeping

A theory is only as good as how much it explains compared with how much it is told. TRPG takes about **28** numbers from measurement. The Standard Model of particles, together with gravity and cosmology, counted the same way, takes about **34**.

TRPG saves three outright: the tau's weight, the down quark's weight and the lightest neutrino's weight. It will save the Higgs weight and two neutrino mixing numbers if pending measurements agree, and one force strength if the strong force lands at 0.1178.

The 28 numbers it still takes from measurement are: the three force strengths; the Higgs level; the scale and twist of the electron's family; five quark weights; four quark-mixing numbers and the strong force's angle; two neutrino spacings; four neutrino mixing numbers that the pattern does not fix; gravity's strength; the amounts of dark energy and dark matter; the matter excess; and the two numbers for the seeds of galaxies.

## The Whole Story in One Breath

In the beginning there was a still, full pond, and a drop fell. Nothing was added; the water that was already there began to spread as waves. In the first fire, everything was relationships, and because a relationship must stay whole with nothing lost, only one kind of number would do. Seen from one spot, facing one way, that number gave three forces, three colours, and every particle of a family with its charge, and the side we see became the side the weak force touches. A far side, hidden like the back of the Moon, leaned on the forces until they met. As the ring cooled, a level settled, and every particle found the place where its push and pull balance: a bubble, with its weight at its edge. The electron's family hung like bells on a 45-degree cone, and the neutrinos sat light on seesaws whose heavy ends hide on the far side. One grain of matter in a billion survived. Quarks joined hands in rings of three, and the proton's size became our window on everything. Bubbles merged into nuclei, the fog lifted, and the web of entanglement drew the universe together as gravity. Stars leaked their light, black holes held theirs, neutron stars merged their bubbles, and galaxies gathered on the faint dimples the drizzle left behind. And here we are, standing on a heap of squished bubbles, seeing one side of one geometry at a time.

Most of that story follows from three ideas, one rule and one bet. The rest is borrowed, with gratitude, from the physicists who came before; measured, where the theory cannot yet reach; and honestly marked as mystery, where the story is still being written.
