# The Quiet at the Top: the Story of Asymptotic Safety

*The idea that gravity needs no new ingredients at all, only a calm, fixed point at the smallest scales, and the theory that predicted the Higgs mass before anyone weighed it.*

---

## Chapter 1. The problem with going small

Every force changes as you look closer. Zoom in on an electron and its electric charge seems to grow, because you push through the cloud of short-lived particles that surrounds it.

Gravity is worse. Zoom in far enough, toward a size about a hundred billion billion times smaller than a proton, and the equations for gravity's strength race toward infinity. Most physicists concluded that Einstein's gravity must break down there and be replaced by something new: strings, loops, or tiny grains of space.

## Chapter 2. A different guess

In 1976 **Steven Weinberg** made a quieter suggestion. Perhaps gravity does not run away at all. Perhaps, as you zoom in, its strength levels off and settles at a fixed value, like a ball rolling into the bottom of a bowl and staying there. If so, nothing breaks. Gravity would be an ordinary quantum force all the way down, with no extra dimensions and no new particles.

He called this **asymptotic safety**: safe because nothing blows up, asymptotic because the calm appears only at the very top of the energy ladder.

For twenty years the idea was hard to test. In 1998 **Martin Reuter** found a way to calculate it, and the calm point appeared in his equations.

## Chapter 3. The bet on the Higgs

In 2009, three years before the Higgs boson was found, **Mikhail Shaposhnikov** and **Christof Wetterich** made a bold bet. If gravity becomes calm at the top, it should also calm the Higgs field. In particular, the Higgs's hold on itself should shrink to almost nothing at the smallest scale. Run that condition back down to everyday energies and it fixes the Higgs mass.

Their answer: about **126 billion electron-volts**, give or take a few.

In July 2012 CERN announced the Higgs boson at **125**.

Very few theories of quantum gravity have ever predicted a number before it was measured. This one did.

## Chapter 4. Since then

**Astrid Eichhorn**, **Aaron Held** and others have extended the idea. They asked whether the same calm could fix the top quark's mass, the strength of electromagnetism, or the difference between the top and bottom quarks. Some results look promising. Others depend on which simplifications are used.

## Chapter 5. The honest doubts

Calculations in asymptotic safety use approximations: they keep some terms and drop others. Critics, including **John Donoghue** in a 2020 review, ask whether the calm point is real or an artefact of the approximation. The idea also does not explain why the particles and forces are what they are. It accepts the Standard Model's list and makes it safe.

### The theory at a glance
| | |
|---|---|
| **Main idea** | Gravity is an ordinary quantum force whose strength settles at a fixed value at the smallest scales |
| **New ingredients** | none: no extra dimensions, no new particles |
| **Measured numbers** | about those of the Standard Model, possibly a few fewer |
| **Got right** | the Higgs mass, predicted in 2009 (about 126) before the 2012 measurement (125) |
| **Doubts** | the approximations; it does not explain the particle list |

### How it differs from TRPG
Asymptotic safety and TRPG are close in spirit. Both stay in four dimensions and add nothing unnecessary. The difference is in what each explains:

- **Asymptotic safety** makes gravity work but takes the particle list as given.
- **TRPG** explains the particle list from relationships but borrows gravity's strength from measurement.

TRPG has **adopted** the Shaposhnikov–Wetterich Higgs rule. Inside TRPG, with its heavy far-side particles included, the rule gives 127.3 ± 1.5 against the measured 125.13 (PDG 2026). That is an agreement at 1.4 standard deviations (2.1 without the 0.5 GeV allowance for the top mass's meaning), needing a top mass 3.7 standard deviations below today's, with the credit for the rule going to its authors.
